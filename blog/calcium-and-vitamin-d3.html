Calcium & Vitamin D3

October 8, 2026
Marilynn Walz

You'll hear things about calcium and vitamin D3 by most pigeon rescues, or at least be advised about it by the pet pigeon community if you decide to adopt a hen. They're both vital to the health of your birds, but it's especially important for hens.


Calcium

Calcium is a mineral often provided in a soluble grit supplement, though supplements exist that provide calcium on its own, or calcium and vitamin D3 combined. Pigeons use calcium for bone health, muscle contraction, nerve function, and eggshell formation. Though a properly formulated diet usually includes calcium, an extra calcium supplement is often required for hens. Eggshells are made of calcium carbonate, which is either supplied via calcium in the diet, or from skeletal reserves.

Calcium deficiency is a much more common issue than oversupplementation. Muscle or bone weakness, lethargy, thin eggshells, and tremors are all possible issues. In more dramatic cases, it can cause metabolic bone disease. As stated before, muscle contraction and nerve function are part of the purpose of calcium, and without it, these can be affected. This is how we get into the issues with calcium deficiency in hens. In excess, it can cause kidney issues as calcium builds up in the body and causes kidney stones.


Vitamin D3

Vitamin D3 is often given via a multivitamin supplement containing the vitamin, a UVB light, or a calcium supplement that contains vitamin D3.

Calcium and vitamin D3 are discussed hand in hand because without vitamin D3, you have no calcium. A pigeon consuming sufficient calcium with insufficient vitamin D3 can still develop a calcium deficiency. Too much phosphorus can bind to calcium and form calcium phosphate, which is insoluble and cannot be broken down and absorbed.

Too much vitamin D3? It's noted by Peter S. Sakas that, "Hypercalcemia occurs, but initially (before calcium levels get too high and with a normal glomerular filtration rate [GFR] by the kidneys) the calcium can be excreted. The GFR will fall, however, when nephrolithiasis (kidney stones) occurs due to prolonged hypercalciuria (high levels of calcium in the urine), producing the elevated blood calcium levels that occur with vitamin D toxicosis. [...] Decreasing calcium in the diet can slow the rate of nephrolithiasis if toxic levels of vitamin D3 have been fed."

However, vitamin D3 is "difficult" to overdose on. Toxicosis has been reported at 30,000 IU/kg or more, and a conservative upper limit of 2800 IU/kg is recommended. A calcium supplement plus a multivitamin at label doses comes nowhere near the toxic level.


How to supplement calcium and vitamin D3

Calcium should be approximately 1% of the diet, with a calcium-phosphorus ratio of 2:1. It can be provided with a grit supplement for most birds. Vitamin D3 is usually present in a good multivitamin or from UVB light. For hens, an additional calcium supplement is required, which can be provided by most avian-specific calcium supplements.

Calcium supplements:

  • Morning Bird Calcium Plus Powder (calcium and vitamin D3)
  • Morning Bird Calcium Plus Liquid (calcium and vitamin D3)
  • Vetafarm Calcivet (calcium and vitamin D3)

Vitamin D3 supplements:

  • A proper UVB light
  • A full-spectrum multivitamin
