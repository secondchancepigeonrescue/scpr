Seeds & Pellets: An Overview

October 6, 2026

Before we start, I am in no way sponsored by a brand in this post. Over the years, pigeon owners have found out what works and what doesn't, and I can be confident in the rescuers I've learned from (and who taught me what I know).

Pigeons are granivores. In the wild and in our homes, seeds make up the main source of their nutrition: protein, fat, and fiber. We won't be covering vegetables or fruits in this article, but you can read more about them here: Greens, Fruits, & Toxic Foods

Despite being a granivore, they do need other dietary supplements (which also won't be covered extensively in this article) since seeds are pretty bad at providing other vitamins and minerals.


Macronutrients

Pigeons need the following macronutrient parameters:

  • Protein: 12-15% (ideally 13-14%)
  • Fat: 2-5% (ideally 3-4%)
  • Fiber: 3-6% (maximum ~9%)

In outdoor lofts where it gets much colder (or if someone is breeding or racing their birds), higher protein and fat can be beneficial. For house pet pigeons, where temperature and environment is (mostly) controlled, extra protein has little benefit. Increased protein has been linked to increased water consumption, increased nitrogen excretion, and wetter droppings. In more dramatic excess, kidney damage and gout have been noted. Less protein does cause poor muscle maintenance, poor molting, and poor feather quality. Weight loss has also been noted.

Sources: Functions of innate and acquired immune system are reduced in domestic pigeons (Columba livia domestica) given a low protein diet, Effects of dietary low protein levels and amino acid patterns on production performance, egg quality and intestinal function in laying hens, Urate Deposition (Gout) in Poultry

Fat is another factor, which is considerably more common in pet pigeons, who are mostly sedentary. In the wild, these birds would be flying to find food, travel from the nest and back, and escape predators. They exert much more energy than our pets (much like how parrots cannot be healthy on a seed-only diet). Pigeons especially love picking out their favorite seeds, which are usually the fattiest. This is also why it's recommended to meal-feed rather than free-feed seeds. In excess, obesity is always a risk. Fatty liver disease can then occur, which can progress into fatty liver haemorrhagic syndrome, or FLHS, where the liver tissue softens and loses its structural integrity, and leads to a higher chance of a rupture or haemorrhage. Too little fat, and your bird's body condition can become poorer, they may be more lethargic, and it can impair the absorption of fat-soluble vitamins.

Sources: Nutritional Disorders of Pet Birds, Management of Pet Birds, Obesity in Birds, Fatty Liver Hemorrhagic Syndrome in Poultry

You'll notice that protein and fat have an ideal percentage, while fiber does not. Fiber isn't a huge factor when it comes to determining pigeon nutrition, but we do try to keep the numbers around 3% to 6%.


Seeds or pellets?

Seeds are the basis of a pigeon's diet, either with seed or grain mixes or seed-/grain-based pellets. Indoor pet pigeons do best on moderate maintenance diets, and the parameters are listed above. These ratios help support healthy body condition, feather quality, immune function, molt cycles, and long-term organ health without promoting excessive weight gain or nutritional imbalance.

There are many seed mixes on the market, and all pigeons will happily eat seeds when it's offered. There are some pickier birds who may not eat one or two seeds from a mix until it's the last bit there, and some who will never touch those one or two types of seeds. Then there's the extremely picky birds who refuse to eat a majority of a mix, and that alone is a bigger concern.

We recommend the following seed mixes for pet pigeons, in no particular order:

  • Des Moines No Corn Regular Pigeon Mix (13%)
  • Versele-Laga Classic 15% No Corn
  • Purgrain Daily 14% POP
  • Purgrain European Supreme Popcorn
  • Brown's Developer Popcorn

We don't particularly have a loving relationship with the Versele-Laga company, however. Their grit brand, Versele-Laga All-In-One, has ingredients that encourage overindulgence and risk impaction among other things. Quality concerns have risen over the last few years as well. The seed mixes are still great, but we would advise using caution when ordering from them considering reports of weevils (pests) and tiny twigs (blockage risk) in new bags.

Pellets are compact, and with the right brand, they provide a balanced, consistent diet while preventing selective feeding. The main disadvantages are mostly management-related, and current veterinary and manufacturer guidance supports properly formulated pigeon pellets as an appropriate long-term and even sole diet for pigeons.

There's a very wild debate about whether pigeons should be on a seed-based diet, pelleted-diet, or a mix of the two. With the current information, evidence suggests that pellets are entirely appropriate for pigeons as a whole diet when properly formulated. I've had a few run-ins with very extreme individuals who claim that pellets are horrible for pigeons and that they can "show neurological issues" and "ruin their digestive systems." It truly does throw me for a loop when I see commentary like this, but I digress.

Firstly, no. Pigeons do not get behavioral problems or "neurological issues" from being on pelleted diets. We see some behavioral issues in parrots who only have one form of food, because a variety in the diet can be mentally stimulating, which parrots require. Pigeons are not that high-strung, and would be content eating safflower seeds and corn all day if we let them (but we don't, and you shouldn't).

The only recommended sole pellet diet for pet pigeons is the Mazuri Checkers Pelleted Pigeon Food, which is within the correct macronutrient range and formulated specifically to be a full diet. Another is the Harrison's Adult Lifetime Superfine, but it must be added in addition to seeds according to instructions.


So what do you feed…?

There is no single right answer. A balanced seed mix with grit and vitamins, a fortified pigeon pellet, or a combination of the two can all keep a pet pigeon healthy. Choose based on your bird's preferences, age, and health, and the amount of cleaning and supplementing you're willing to manage. When in doubt, your avian vet is the best guide.
