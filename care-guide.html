Care Guide

caring for your pigeon

Introduction

As pigeon owners, pigeon lovers, and rescuers, all of whom dedicate their time to learning more about these animals, it's our duty to provide the best possible care we can. This means not only advocating for them, but also practicing the advice we give to others. Pigeons are our responsibility. Advocating for them starts with learning about what they need.

Understand that while studies on pigeons do exist, many of them focus specifically on racing pigeons and treat pigeons as livestock rather than pets. Pet pigeon owners need to apply their findings with some nuance. Much information discussed by the pet pigeon community is often anecdotal. In addition, there are pet pigeon influencers who have in the past spread misinformation regarding pet pigeon care, which many took as fact, repeated, and used as the basis for their approach to pigeon care. Do your research. Use any advice, including ours, at your own discretion.

This is a new and improved guide from our previous “SCPR’s Guide to Pigeon Care.” We expect adopters to meet the minimum care requirements at all times.

Housing

Where to put your cage

There are three options for housing your pigeons: indoors in a cage, indoors in a whole room, and outdoors. Most people adopt one or two pet pigeons and keep them in an indoor enclosure. For that reason, our guide covers indoor enclosures, not outdoor ones.

If you choose to go for a whole room (or multiple rooms) for your birds, they should have adequate space and the room should be bird-proofed. Ceiling fans should never be turned on in the room, windows should be secure and closed at all times, and all indoor rooms where your pigeon roams freely should have a true HEPA air purifier rated for a larger room than it’s in to keep your lungs safe.

If you decide to go for a cage, make sure it isn’t in a direct draft or having more than just a few hours of sunlight directly in front of a window. Sunlight through a window provides no usable UVB light. Placing them in front of a window to get them “more sunlight” is ineffective and can be dangerous.

Keep in mind that while a pair of pigeons can keep each other company, a single pigeon needs a primary caretaker who can hang out for as long as possible per day and should be housed close to them.

Enclosure

Enclosures should prioritize floor space, since pigeons are more walking and ground foraging, not climbing like parrots. Your birds should be kept in the following:

  • Minimum recommendation for adopters: At least 8 square feet for one or two pigeons and 12 square feet for large-breed pairs (such as Kings, giant runts, and Modenas).
  • We recommend at least 10 square feet for a single large-breed pigeon or a pair of average-sized pigeons.
  • We do not recommend opting for a smaller cage unless you can give them additional free flight. If you opt for 6-square-foot cages (the absolute bare minimum), your bird must have at least six hours of free-roam time to make up for the difference in cage space.

Dog kennels come in 42-inch, 48-inch, and larger sizes, which correspond closely with our square footage recommendations and provide a little more perching opportunities. Your pigeon will be able to fly to higher perches, flutter down to the ground, and show more natural behavior.

  • For single birds: Midwest 42" Dog Crate
  • For pairs: Midwest 48" Dog Crate
  • For large breed pairs: Midwest 52" Dog Crate

C&C cages can be customized into different shapes and sizes, and more panels can be added later. Some cat C&C cages come with extra perching spots. These take a little more setup but can provide more space than many other cages.

  • OneLuck Cat Cage 55"x55"x55". Made for cats, sturdier than using storage C&C panels, and 21 square feet of floor space.

Enclosed playpens have the added benefit of being lightweight, faster to set up than C&C cages, and many are washable. It’s much harder to add perches up high, however, and many are either too short or too small to be used as a permanent cage without more free roam time.

  • For single birds: Aivituvin Foldable Playpen (L)
  • For single large breeds and all pairs: Aivituvin Foldable Playpen (XL)

Critter Nations are an acceptable predator-proofed option and can be made wider by putting another cage on top or removing a wall and adding it to the side. To be properly predator-proofed, they must have ½” bar spacing (not 1”).

Parrot flight cages are often too small for pigeons. They’re usually not wide enough and prioritize flying space, which a pigeon won’t prefer. If you have a large parrot flight cage made for larger parrots, they can work wonders for a sturdier and more easily sanitized enclosure.

Predator-proofing

If you have cats, dogs, parrots, or other animals, your cage should be predator-proofed with ½” to ¼” 16-gauge wire mesh, also known as hardware cloth, on all sides of their enclosure: top, sides, and bottom. Cats especially have been known to reach a paw in and severely injure pigeons, even if they can’t get direct access with their mouths.

Cage essentials

Cage liners can include disposable and washable options. Pigeons do not need bedding like sand, dirt, wood shavings etc. for enclosures (both indoors and outdoors).

  • Washable: washable puppy pads, low-pile blankets like fleece, yoga mats, contact paper.
  • Disposable: puppy pads, bird cage liners.

Perches should be provided. At least a few flat perches are required, as pigeons are naturally cliff-dwellers and prefer to roost and stand on flat surfaces. We recommend at least 2+ flat perches for singles and 3+ perches for flat pairs. You can add other perches in addition if you have the space, including natural branch perches, rope perches, and dowel perches. We recommend removing dowel perches if your pigeon begins having foot issues (like bumblefoot) from preferring to stand on them.

A hide can double as a nesting area and an area for your bird to go if they want to get away from prying eyes. Look into large guinea pig or rabbit hides, cat hidey houses, or wooden boxes turned on their side. You can add an additional cat bed or small dog bed. In taller enclosures and in the room, cat towers provide more variety and often include a hidey area!

A full-spectrum UVB light is beneficial to both sexes and very important if your bird is a female. It can be placed about 8” to 12” above a perch, provided for 12 hours a day, and should be placed only above, not to the side, so your bird has the option to move away from the UVB light.

There are more enrichment and toy items you can add to your pigeon's cage, which we detail later. Do not use mirrors. We default to saying that mirrors are harmful, as they can become a place of obsession. The study that says pigeons can self-recognize trained those pigeons to do so. Pigeons do not automatically believe their reflection is themselves!

Diet and Nutrition

Seeds & Pellets

Pigeons are naturally granivores, eating seeds, grains, and legumes as the main part of their diet, especially in captivity. We must also supplement them with a multivitamin and soluble grit since seeds don't contain enough vitamins and minerals to complete their nutritional requirements.

If this sounds scary, don't worry. Fifty-pound seed bags (the best economically) can last you over a year, and most multivitamins and soluble grit supplements can last you many months, if not a year.

For the ideal macronutrient ratio, you want a protein percentage of 13-14% and a fat percentage of 3-4%. Fiber doesn't have a specific sweet spot, but keep it under 9% maximum.

  • Protein: 12% to 15%
  • Fat: 2% to 5%
  • Fiber: 3% to 6%

We recommend meal-feeding instead of free feeding. Pigeons know what seeds they like and will pick out all their favorites instead of eating everything as they should. Some pigeons will usually leave one or two seed types and eat the rest.

You can start by feeding 1 tablespoon per 200 grams of body weight, or start out with 2 tablespoons. If the bird eats all of the seed by the time you go to bed (with none left), increase the amount by about half a tablespoon. If they're leaving a lot of their seed behind, decrease by about the remaining amount.

We recommend any of the following:

  • Des Moines No Corn Regular Pigeon Mix (13%)
  • Brown's Developer Popcorn
  • Purgrain European Supreme Popcorn
  • Purgrain Daily 14% POP
  • Versele-Laga Classic 15% No Corn

Some owners opt to feed their bird a pelleted diet. Pellets can provide balanced nutrition and fortified vitamin content and negate the pickiness that pigeons have with different seed types, though they can be a bit picky about pellet size. Different brands have different pellet mixes, some of which are meant to be used as a supplement, not as the base of the whole diet.

There are dozens of pellets on the market, but only two are recommended, and only one is proper as a whole diet:

  • Mazuri Checkers Pelleted Pigeon Food is a complete diet. No multivitamin is needed, though we recommend supplementing a good soluble grit to both sexes.
  • Harrison's Adult Lifetime Fine is a good supplement to add to an already existing seed mix. It is not meant to be a full diet for pet pigeons and must be used as a supplement.

Pigeons also do not need fruits or vegetables to be healthy. They can make great enrichment items, but don't exceed veggies in more than 10% of their diet. Fruits should be treated as treats. Most fruits and veggies safe for parrots are also safe for pigeons.

Multivitamins & Grit

A soluble grit and multivitamin supplement are vital for getting trace vitamins and minerals to your pigeon.

Some sources claim pigeons don't need grit. Pigeons need soluble grit supplements, not straight insoluble grit. Insoluble grit is responsible for mechanical grinding in the gizzard. Soluble grit is more of a mineral supplement, but many soluble grit products contain some insoluble content for digestion.

You can offer it freely in a separate dish, or sprinkle a well-sized pinch over their food 2 to 3 times per week.

  • Morning Bird Pigeon Grit
  • Jedd's Redstone Natural Pigeon Grit
  • Des Moines Pigeon Grit & Mineral Plus
  • Versele-Laga Grit w/ Redstone

Multivitamins can be dosed 2 to 3 times per week and are usually added to water or coated on their seeds with a pigeon-safe oil, such as olive oil. These can also be dosed 2 or 3 times per week.

  • Zoo Med Avian Plus
  • Lafeber Avi-Era
  • Nekton-S

Other Supplements

Laying hens require a calcium and vitamin D3 supplement. Though they get calcium and vitamin D3 from a soluble grit and multivitamin, it usually isn't enough to support egg-laying on its own. Vitamin D3 is required to utilize calcium. Otherwise, the calcium goes nowhere.

Some supplements have both calcium and vitamin D3, such as Morning Bird Calcium Plus (which comes as a powder and a water additive). You can also offer a generic calcium supplement while providing your birds with a UVB lamp.

There aren't too many other supplements you need to give your pigeons. Many other supplements give extra nutrients for breeding or racing birds, which our pet pigeons don't need. Some people use apple cider vinegar (ACV) in water and claim it helps with gut health.

Free-roaming & Enrichment

Free-roaming

Pigeons need at least 4 hours of free-roaming time outside their cage every day. Even a large cage limits how much a pigeon can walk, fly, and explore, and daily time out helps keep them physically fit, mentally stimulated, and bonded with you. Supervise your pigeon during free-roam time. If you set up a fully bird-proofed room for longer, unsupervised stretches, remove every hazard and check the room as often as you can.

Because pigeons spend a lot of time on the floor, watch where you step and sit, check before closing doors, and avoid recliners and folding furniture while your pigeon is out. Keep your pigeon completely separated from dogs, cats, ferrets, and other pets, no matter how well-behaved they seem. One accident is enough to lose your bird. This includes other birds: parrots can badly injure pigeons, and pigeons can injure doves and small parrots.

Choose a room or space where doors and windows can be securely closed, and make the same quick safety check every time you open the cage.

  • Ceiling fans should be turned off, doors and windows should be closed or have a very secure screen over openings, and mirrors should be covered. Glass is also a high collision risk, so add either curtains or decals to them.
  • Open water like buckets of mop water, open toilets, and filled sinks or tubs are a drowning risk. Kitchens should not be free-roamed in due to burn and fume risk.
  • Avoid candles, incense, air fresheners, aerosol sprays, and harsh cleaners in the pigeon’s airspace. A good rule of thumb is that if you can smell it, it may be a danger to your bird.
  • If your bird likes to bite at plants, keep toxic houseplants away and in another room.

Pigeons cannot be potty trained. Instead, you can use washable covers on furniture, puppy pads or easy-to-clean flooring in favorite pooping spots, and pigeon pants (flight suits with a removable liner) for time on couches or beds.

Pigeon pants should be fitted properly and changed every few hours. They can only be worn for about three hours maximum per day. Pigeons who are broody and ready to lay eggs or are sick or injured should not wear pigeon pants.

Clean droppings regularly; dried droppings add to airborne dust, which makes a good air purifier even more important in rooms where your pigeon roams.

Nest building

There is one absolutely required enrichment tool: nest building. Providing a nesting spot and adequate nesting materials is extremely important, especially for hens. It can help you bond more closely with your pigeon when you bring them small bits to use in their nest.

Additionally, hens must have a nest and be allowed to lay. Hens should never have their nests destroyed or eggs taken away (without replacing them with fake eggs, also known as feggs). Pigeons are not seasonal layers like parrots and lay eggs every month. They will also lay immediately when eggs are destroyed or taken, which uses more calcium than normal. This can lead to health issues such as egg binding, calcium deficiency, chronic egg laying, and metabolic bone disorder.

Both male and female pigeons enjoy nest building, not just hens!

Toys

Some pigeons like toys! Crinkly cat toys, cat bell balls or spring toys, parrot toys with bells, jingly bits, and easily grabbed bits to tug on, sola balls, and other natural parrot toys. Many parrot toys not safe for parrots are considered safe for pigeons. Avoid toys with small holes that they can get their small beaks and feet stuck in. Not every pigeon likes toys! Don't go all out on toys until you find something your pigeon likes.

Foraging

Another great option is to get a foraging mat or a new and clean chenille bathroom mat. You can sprinkle a portion of their usual seed mix over it on days when their seeds aren't covered in supplements.

Dig boxes can be put in their enclosure with a mix of nesting materials, seeds, and toys for a mix of nesting material and foraging enrichment.

Training

Training is a great way to enrich your pigeon's life! You can start with basic cue words to get them used to your voice and condition them to be calm and expect your actions: one for cleaning in the cage, seed, water, “excuse me” when you walk by, and more. Some people also train a “goodnight” or “go home” command, so their birds learn to go home on their own.

For other commands, tricks, and useful behaviors, you usually need to be somewhat bonded with your bird, and they should be comfortable eating a treat from your hand.

Start by associating a cue with you feeding them a treat, like a safflower seed. Slowly, you can work on basic commands like “step up,” a very useful command for moving your pigeon from room to room. This can progress into a recall cue (only indoors; never free-fly your pigeon!).

You can also teach them other tricks, like how to spin! Get creative with what you teach. Pigeons have been able to guide missiles and identify cancer in pictures!

Air purifier

Pigeons are very dusty birds: their feathers shed a fine powder, and their dander and dried droppings also become airborne. Breathing in enough of this dust over time can lead to hypersensitivity pneumonitis, also called bird fancier’s lung.

Filtration

A good air purifier uses three- or four-stage filtration: a pre-filter, an activated carbon filter, and a true HEPA filter. Pre-filters catch larger particles (hair, lint, carpet fibers, small down feathers). Activated charcoal traps gas molecules like odors from pets, and can help with fumes, chemicals, and VOCs. You still should not have items that give off fumes, chemicals, or VOCs by pigeons regardless. A true HEPA filter captures at least 99.97% of particles that are 0.3 microns in size.

Be wary of products advertising “HEPA-type,” “HEPA-like,” or “HEPA-style” filtration. These terms are unregulated marketing language with no testing or certification behind them. Look specifically for “True HEPA” or a grade such as H13.

Sizing

Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR) measures how much filtered air a purifier delivers per minute, in cubic feet per minute (CFM). Because pigeons are so dusty, buy a purifier rated for a larger room than you have. If your bird room is 100 square feet, choose one rated for at least 120 to 130 square feet. Rooms with ceilings above 8 feet also need a larger unit.

Dangerous air purifiers

Avoid any air purifier that uses an ionizer, ozone generator, “plasma,” or “active oxygen” technology. These produce ozone, which irritates human lungs and can be deadly to birds even in small amounts. Avoid purifiers with fragrance or aromatherapy features, as scented products are unsafe around birds.

If your air purifier mentions an ionizer, anion setting, or ozone emission limits, choose a different model.

Using an air purifier

  • Run it continuously, not only when the room looks dusty. Most airborne dust can’t be seen.
  • Place it a few feet from the cage with nothing blocking the intake, but don’t point the airflow directly at your pigeons.
  • Replace the HEPA and carbon filters on the manufacturer’s schedule, or sooner. Pigeon dust fills filters faster than typical household dust.
  • An air purifier works alongside cleaning; it doesn’t replace it. Wipe surfaces with a damp cloth instead of dry dusting, use a vacuum with a HEPA filter, clean droppings regularly, and open windows (with a screen) for fresh air when weather allows. Consider wearing a mask when cleaning the cage.

If you develop a persistent cough, shortness of breath, or flu-like symptoms that improve when you’re away from your birds, see a doctor.

Cleaning

Cleaning basics

The places your free-roaming pigeon is should be spot-cleaned throughout the day, especially its poop, with basic wipe-downs, sweeping, and vacuuming done daily or every other day. Small daily cleanups are easier to clean than letting things progress and build up.

Always use bird-safe cleaners, and move your pigeons out of the room during heavy cleaning. Let the area air out and dry fully before bringing them back. Never mix bleach with vinegar, ammonia, or other cleaners, as this can create toxic gases.

Never use air fresheners, candles (even ones labeled “bird-safe” or “pet-safe”), perfumes, aerosol sprays, incense, or essential oil diffusers around pigeons. Their fumes can cause breathing problems or death.

Cleaning tools

Look for vacuums with HEPA filters and strong suction. Seeds, grit, and feathers can clog weaker vacuums, so wet/dry shop vacuums and strong uprights tend to work best. Avoid vacuums with Teflon or nonstick parts. Steam cleaners are useful because they disinfect with heat instead of chemicals.

Enzyme cleaners break down droppings and food residue instead of covering up smells. Use these for everyday cleaning of cages, perches, and floors. A commonly used enzyme cleaner is Nature’s Miracle Bird Cage & Surface Cleaner Spray. A disinfectant should also be used, like F10 Veterinary Disinfectant or REScue One-Step.

There are some homemade spray cleaners you can use as well:

  • Vinegar: 1:1 ratio with water for most surfaces
  • Hydrogen peroxide: 1:1 ratio with water for flooring; spot test first
  • Baking soda: sprinkle on carpet to deodorize, then vacuum

Unscented bleach should be used with caution. Remove birds from the room, and mist down with plain water afterwards and wipe down. Do not let your birds roam the room or be in the room until after the bleach smell has completely subsided and it's been aired out for ~1 hour.

Droppings

Droppings should be wiped up when possible, and always throughout the day to minimize poop in their (and your) living space. Dried droppings can be soaked for ~10 minutes with a cleaner to make them easier to clean up.

Feathers

Feather dust is a normal part of owning pigeons, but ignoring it can lead to respiratory issues. Pigeons molt year-round and usually have one or two heavier molts each year.

  • Pick up large feathers by hand.
  • Vacuum or sweep smaller feathers and down.
  • Wipe surfaces with a damp cloth instead of dry dusting.
  • Clean vents, fans, and fabrics regularly, since dust collects there quickly.
  • Run an air purifier in rooms where your pigeons spend time.
  • Offer a shallow bath at least once a week, ideally several times. Bathing can help reduce dust at the source.
  • During heavy molts, clean once or twice a day instead of every other day.

Deep cleaning

  • At least twice a week, deep clean your bird’s cage. Remove everything from the cage and move it to clean underneath. Clear debris from corners, bars, trays, and tray rails. Use an enzyme cleaner on stuck droppings and follow up with a disinfectant. Let everything dry before putting it back together.
  • Soak perches, toys, and bowls in warm, soapy water, scrub, rinse, and disinfect with a bird-safe disinfectant. Wipe off any leftover cleaner and dry fully before returning them.
  • Hand-wash fabric hides in hot water with unscented detergent. If heavily soiled, soak for 10 minutes in diluted bleach, then follow up with a proper hot water and unscented detergent wash.
  • Steam mop hard floors every 1 to 2 weeks using hot water and a little white vinegar. For carpet, vacuum with a HEPA vacuum and use a carpet cleaner or wet vacuum every few weeks.
  • Machine-wash curtains, blankets, and clothing, adding a small amount of bleach if needed. Dust blinds often and wipe both sides with a damp cloth.

Cleaning different materials

  • Treated wood: Wipe with vinegar solution or bird-safe cleaner, then disinfect.
  • Untreated wood: Absorbs waste and odors easily. Spot clean often, and replace pieces with stains that won’t come out.
  • Fabric: Remove loose debris, then spot clean with vinegar solution or bird-safe cleaner.
  • Leather: Wipe with a mild cleaner or vinegar solution and dry thoroughly to prevent cracking.
